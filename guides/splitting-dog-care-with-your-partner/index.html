How to split dog care with your partner

Updated 2026-10-26 · 8 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Most dog-care resentment is not about hours. It is about who carries the mental load — the person who notices the food is low, books the vet, and remembers the tablet is the one doing the invisible work, and “just ask me and I'll do it” makes them the manager rather than a partner. Split ownership of whole jobs, not tasks handed out on request, and rotate the unpopular slots.

Why it feels unfair when the hours look equal

Two people can spend identical minutes on the dog and have completely different experiences of it, because dog care has two layers.

The visible layer is walks, meals, baths — things you can point at and count. The invisible layer is noticing that the food is nearly out, knowing the flea treatment is due this week, booking the vet, remembering the tablet is with breakfast and not dinner, noticing she has been quiet today, arranging cover for the Thursday you are both out.

The invisible layer is where the resentment lives, and it is almost never on the chore chart. One partner can do 50% of the walks and 0% of the noticing, and genuinely, sincerely believe the split is even — because from where they stand it is.

“Just tell me what needs doing and I'll do it” sounds generous. It assigns one person permanent responsibility for knowing what needs doing, which is the harder half of the job.

Research on dogs in couples has found roughly a third of people report dog care regularly taking time away from their relationship, so the friction is common rather than a sign that something is wrong with yours.

The conversation, in four steps

1. Write down everything, together

Every job, including the invisible ones: ordering food, booking vet appointments, flea and worm treatment, nail trims, washing the bed, arranging cover, buying the next bag before it runs out. Most households find twenty-plus items and are surprised by the list. Do this part without assigning anything — the list on its own usually ends the argument about whether the split is even.

2. Mark who currently does each one

Current reality, not intentions. This is the step that tends to be uncomfortable and the one that does the work.

3. Reassign whole jobs, not tasks

The crucial distinction. “You do the evening walk” is a task. “You own evening walks” means you also own noticing it is 8pm, deciding what happens when it rains, and arranging cover when you are out. Transferring tasks keeps the mental load where it was. Transferring ownership moves it.

4. Agree what happens when it fails

Not if — when. Does the other person cover silently? Does the walk get skipped? Is there a swap? Deciding this in advance, calmly, prevents the 9pm argument where one person is tired and the other feels accused.

Three splits that hold up

ModelHow it works SuitsFails when
Split by time of day One owns mornings end to end, the other owns evenings Different work schedules; clean ownership boundaries One slot is much harder than the other and never rotates
Alternate whole days Everything about the dog is yours on your day Shift work, and people who dislike constant negotiation Days off fall unevenly; needs an explicit swap mechanism
Split by job type One owns feeding and vet admin, the other owns walks and grooming Genuinely different preferences and capabilities The admin half is invisible and therefore undercounted

Whichever you choose, build in two things: rotate the worst slot (the early walk, the rainy evening, the yard), and make swaps explicit. A swap that is asked for and accepted is fine. A swap that is assumed is where resentment starts.

If one person wanted the dog more. This is often the unspoken subtext, and it deserves saying out loud rather than being litigated through the walk rota. “I didn't want a dog” is a real position, but it does not change the dog's needs, and relitigating it every Tuesday is worse for everyone than agreeing a proportionate but genuine share.

What makes the invisible work visible

Three things, in order of how much difference they make:

  1. Write the list. Once, properly, including the admin. Nothing else on this page matters as much.
  2. Make completion visible. Not to police each other — to stop the checking. Most of the friction in a shared household is not the work, it is having to ask whether the work happened, and being asked.
  3. Give reminders an owner. A reminder that goes to both people goes to neither. One that goes to whoever is on duty, and escalates if nothing happens, removes the need for one person to be the household's alarm clock.

That last point is the specific thing Arrow was built for, and it is worth being clear about why: a reminder everyone receives is a reminder nobody owns.

Common questions

How do couples split dog responsibilities?

The three models that hold up are splitting by time of day, alternating whole days, or splitting by job type. All three work better than handing out tasks on request, because they transfer ownership rather than labour.

My partner doesn't help with the dog. What do I do?

Start with the list rather than the complaint — write every job down together, including the invisible admin, and mark who does each. Most people genuinely do not realise the distribution until they see it. Then reassign whole jobs, not tasks, and agree in advance what happens when one is missed.

Is it fair for one person to do all the dog care?

If both people chose the dog, no. If one person is at home all day while the other commutes, an equal split of hours may not be the right target — but an equal split of the mental load usually is, and that is the part most often left entirely with one person.

How do we handle it when one of us forgets?

Decide the protocol in advance, when nobody is annoyed: who covers, whether it is swapped or simply done, and whether it gets mentioned at all. The argument is almost never about the missed walk; it is about not having agreed what a missed walk means.
