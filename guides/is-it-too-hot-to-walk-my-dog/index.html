Is it too hot to walk my dog?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 6 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Press the back of your hand flat on the pavement for seven seconds. If you cannot hold it there comfortably, it is too hot for paws. Surfaces in direct sun run roughly 40–60°F (20–30°C) above air temperature, so an 86°F / 30°C day can mean 135°F / 57°C asphalt — hot enough to burn pads in about a minute. Walk early morning or late evening instead.

The maths people get wrong

The mistake is checking the weather app instead of the ground. Air temperature and surface temperature are not close to the same thing. Asphalt in direct sun absorbs and holds heat, and runs far hotter than the air above it.

Approximate asphalt temperatures in direct sun. Paw pads can burn at roughly 120–125°F (49–52°C).
Air temperatureAsphalt in direct sun Verdict
77°F / 25°C~125°F / 52°C Already at the burn threshold. Test before you go
86°F / 30°C~135°F / 57°C Burns pads in roughly 60 seconds. Grass only
95°F / 35°C~150°F / 65°C No pavement walking at all

Note the first row: a pleasant 77°F afternoon is already marginal. This is why the seven-second test matters more than any threshold — it measures the actual surface, in the actual sun, at the actual hour. The test is endorsed by veterinary sources including Washington State University's veterinary college and the American Animal Hospital Association.

Who is most at risk

  • Puppies and small dogs. Closer to the radiating ground, and softer pads.
  • Flat-faced breeds — bulldogs, pugs, boxers, Frenchies. Dogs cool themselves mainly by panting, and a shortened airway makes that far less effective. These breeds overheat at temperatures other dogs tolerate easily.
  • Overweight dogs, who carry more insulation and less cardiovascular reserve.
  • Seniors, and dogs with heart or respiratory conditions.
  • Thick double-coated breeds — huskies, malamutes, German shepherds.

Walking in hot weather, practically

  • Shift walks to before 8am and after sunset. The ground needs hours to cool, so 7pm on a hot day can still be too hot
  • Walk on grass, dirt or shaded paths rather than pavement
  • Carry water and offer it every ten to fifteen minutes
  • Shorten the walk and replace the lost exercise with indoor sniffing games — mental work tires dogs out more than people expect
  • Never leave a dog in a parked car, not for two minutes, not with the windows cracked
  • Check pads afterwards: redness, darkened patches, limping or licking all suggest a burn

Heatstroke is an emergency. Heavy frantic panting, thick drool, bright red or very pale gums, wobbling or stumbling, vomiting, or collapse. Move the dog into shade, wet them with cool — not ice-cold — water, offer small drinks, and drive to a vet immediately while cooling continues. Do not wait to see if it improves. Heatstroke can be fatal within an hour and dogs continue to deteriorate even after being moved out of the heat.

The household angle

Hot weather is when walk schedules change — and when a change in the schedule has real consequences. The 2pm walk moves to 6am, which means the person who normally does 2pm is not the one doing it. The classic hot-day failure is that the dog gets no walk at all, because the morning person assumed the evening person would take the shifted slot.

Agree the summer schedule once, as a household, rather than renegotiating it by text every morning during a heatwave.

Common questions

At what temperature is it too hot to walk a dog?

There is no single safe air temperature, because surface heat depends on sun, material and time of day. The AKC's chief veterinarian flags 85°F / 29°C and above as the point where pavement becomes risky, but at 77°F / 25°C in full sun asphalt can already reach burn temperature. Test the ground, not the forecast.

Do dog boots help?

They protect the pads, if your dog tolerates them and they fit properly. They do nothing about overheating, which is the more dangerous risk — boots can make a hot walk feel safe when it is not.

Should I shave my double-coated dog in summer?

Generally no. A double coat insulates against heat as well as cold and shields the skin from sunburn; shaving it can make a dog hotter and the coat often grows back poorly. Brush out the dead undercoat instead, and ask your vet or a groomer about your specific breed.
