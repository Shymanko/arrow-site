How much should I feed my dog, by weight?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 8 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

A rough starting point is 25–30 kcal per pound (55–65 kcal per kg) of body weight per day for an average adult dog. The more accurate method, and the one vets use, is RER = 70 × kg0.75, multiplied by a life-stage factor — 1.6 for a neutered adult. Then convert those calories into grams using the kcal/kg figure on your own bag, and adjust by what your dog's body actually does over the next month.

Why calories don’t scale in a straight line

Notice that a 100 lb dog needs about 1,960 kcal while a 50 lb dog needs 1,160 — not half. That is the 0.75 exponent at work. Metabolic rate scales with body surface area rather than mass, so doubling a dog's weight does not double its energy needs. Any chart that scales linearly is overfeeding your big dog, your small dog, or both.

The gap between the top and bottom rows is more than double. This is why “how much should I feed a 20 kg dog” has no single answer, and why the chart on the bag — which knows only the weight — is necessarily a compromise.

It is also why neutering so often leads to quiet weight gain. The requirement drops by roughly 11% on the day of the operation; the bowl usually does not.

Stop measuring in cups

Two problems, and they compound. First, foods differ: dry dog foods range from about 300 to 500 kcal per 100 g, so the same volume can differ by a third in calories. Second, people differ: the same scoop filled by three members of the same household varies by 20–30% depending on how hard it is packed and whether it is levelled.

In a household where more than one person feeds the dog, a kitchen scale will do more for your dog's weight than any change of food. Weigh the portion once, mark the level on a cup with a marker pen, and everybody is now feeding the same amount.

How to adjust, and how often

The equation gives you week one. Your dog gives you the rest. Published guidance is consistent that individual requirements vary by as much as 50% either side of the typical figure for a given weight, driven by metabolism, activity, breed and neuter status.

  1. Feed the calculated amount for two to three weeks.
  2. Weigh the dog and run a hands-on body condition check.
  3. Ribs getting hard to feel, waist disappearing? Reduce by about 10%.
  4. Ribs becoming sharply visible, waist very pronounced? Increase by about 10%.
  5. Wait another two to three weeks before changing again.

Weekly adjustments lead to overcorrection. Dogs do not respond to a new portion size overnight, and day-to-day weight fluctuates with gut contents and hydration.

Why this is worth getting right

Purina's 14-year study followed 48 Labradors from seven litters. One dog in each matched pair was fed 25% less than its littermate, on the same food. The lean-fed dogs lived a median of 13.0 years against 11.2 — 1.8 years longer — and did not need treatment for age-related chronic conditions until 12.0 years, against 9.9 in the control group.

That is the clearest evidence available that portion size is not a cosmetic question. Meanwhile, the Association for Pet Obesity Prevention found 59% of US dogs overweight or obese, while only 17% of owners described their dog that way. Almost nobody thinks it is their dog.

Feeding one dog with four people

The arithmetic above assumes the dog eats what you decided to feed it. In a shared household it frequently does not — not because anyone is careless, but because a second breakfast and a handful of unlogged treats from three different people never look like overfeeding to any individual. They just show up on the scale three months later.

Common questions

How much should I feed a 20 kg dog?

About 1,060 kcal a day if they are a neutered adult at a healthy weight — 70 × 200.75 × 1.6. In grams that depends entirely on your food's calorie density: roughly 265 g of a 400 kcal/100 g food, or 350 g of a 300 kcal/100 g one.

Is it better to feed once or twice a day?

Twice suits most adult dogs and is the standard recommendation. Deep-chested breeds in particular should not get one large daily meal. Puppies need three to four meals depending on age.

Should I feed by weight or by the chart on the bag?

Start with the equation rather than the bag, because the bag knows only your dog's weight and not whether they are neutered, elderly or a working dog. Then let body condition override both.

My dog is always hungry — should I feed more?

Persistent hunger on an adequate ration is worth a vet conversation; several conditions cause it. In the meantime, bulk without calories helps: splitting the same ration into more meals, adding a little plain green bean or plain pumpkin, and feeding from a puzzle feeder so the meal takes fifteen minutes instead of forty seconds.
