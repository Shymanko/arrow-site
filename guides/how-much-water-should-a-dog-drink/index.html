How much water should a dog drink a day?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 5 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

The working baseline is about 1 fl oz per pound of body weight per day (roughly 50–60 ml per kg) — so around 30 fl oz for a 30 lb dog. Heat, exercise, dry food, puppyhood and nursing all push it up legitimately. Sustained intake above roughly 100 ml per kg per day is considered abnormal and is a reason to see a vet.

What normal looks like

Approximate daily intake at roughly 1 fl oz per lb. Individual variation is wide — some veterinary references give a range as broad as 0.3 to 1.7 fl oz per lb depending on diet and conditions.

Reasons a dog legitimately drinks more

  • Dry food versus wet. The single biggest dietary factor. Wet food is around 75–80% water, so dogs on it drink visibly less from the bowl and dogs switched from wet to dry drink dramatically more. That is not a new problem; it is the same water arriving by a different route.
  • Heat and exercise. Both raise losses through panting.
  • Puppies drink more relative to size while growing.
  • Pregnancy and nursing raise requirements substantially.
  • Some medications — steroids and diuretics in particular — cause marked increases. Expected, but worth confirming with your vet so you know what is normal for your dog.

Increased drinking plus increased urinating is one of the most important early symptom pairs in dogs. It is a classic early sign of diabetes, kidney disease and Cushing's — all of which are far more manageable caught early. If you notice the water bowl emptying faster than it used to, and more trips outside, book an appointment rather than waiting for something more obvious. Measuring the bowl for two or three days before you go gives your vet something concrete to work with.

Drinking much less than usual

A sudden drop matters too. Common causes include nausea, dental pain making drinking uncomfortable, a recent switch from dry food to wet, or a water bowl in a location the dog avoids — next to a noisy appliance, or near another pet's space.

Check hydration directly: lift the skin between the shoulder blades and let go. It should spring straight back. Slow return, along with tacky or dry gums, suggests dehydration, which combined with not drinking is a same-day vet call — particularly in a puppy or an older dog.

Practical things that help

  • More than one bowl, in different rooms, especially in a multi-level home
  • Fresh water daily — wash the bowl, don't just top it up; biofilm builds quickly
  • Water available on walks in warm weather, offered every 10–15 minutes
  • A second bowl where an older dog spends most of its time, to avoid stairs
  • Ceramic or stainless steel rather than plastic, which scratches and holds bacteria

Measuring it when four people refill the bowl

If your vet asks how much your dog drinks in a day, the honest answer in most households is nobody knows — whoever notices it is low tops it up, and no one is counting. For two or three days before an appointment, measure what goes in with a jug and note refills. That turns “she seems to be drinking a lot” into a number, which is far more useful to a vet than an impression.

Common questions

How much water should a dog drink per day in ml?

Roughly 50–60 ml per kg of body weight. A 20 kg dog therefore drinks around 1–1.2 litres a day on dry food, less on wet.

Why is my dog suddenly drinking a lot of water?

Heat, more exercise or a switch to dry food explain many cases. If none of those apply, or the change persists for more than a few days, see a vet — increased thirst is an early sign of diabetes, kidney disease and Cushing's, and all three are much more manageable when caught early.

Should I limit my dog's water at night?

Only for a puppy in house-training, and only overnight. Adult dogs should have water available at all times, and any dog that is unwell, in hot weather, or on medication must not have water restricted. If you think your dog is drinking too much, the answer is a vet appointment, not a smaller bowl.
