Missed a dose of your dog’s medication?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

The general rule: if you remember within a few hours, give it. If you are already more than halfway to the next dose, skip it and resume the normal schedule. Never double up to catch up. That rule has important exceptions — insulin, seizure medication and heartworm prevention each have their own — so call your vet, who can answer in a minute.

First: do not panic, and do not double

A single missed dose is rarely a disaster, and the most common harm from a missed dose comes from the correction rather than the omission. Doubling up to “catch up” is genuinely dangerous with several common veterinary drugs.

Call your practice. They can tell you in under a minute what to do for your specific drug, and what matters is the combination of which medication, how long since the missed dose, and how long until the next one.

Where the general rule does not apply

Guidance differs by drug class. This is orientation for the phone call, not a substitute for it.
MedicationWhat to know
Insulin Never give extra or double without speaking to your vet — it risks hypoglycaemia, which is an emergency. Call before acting
Seizure medication Consistent blood levels are the whole point. Call your vet the same day; abrupt gaps can trigger seizures
Heartworm prevention Less than two weeks late: give it now. More than two weeks: give it and call — most preventives have roughly a 45-day retroactive window, and your vet will likely want a heartworm test about six months later
Antibiotics Give the missed dose if the next is not imminent, then continue. Your vet may extend the course. Finishing the full course matters more than any single dose
Heart medication Give it when you remember unless it is nearly time for the next. Do not double. Call if more than one dose was missed
Steroids Never stop abruptly and never alter the taper on your own. Call
Pain relief (NSAIDs) Skip the missed dose and resume. Doubling NSAIDs risks stomach ulceration and kidney injury

Call immediately, rather than waiting, if your dog has been given a double dose of anything, if more than one dose of a critical medication has been missed, or if they are showing symptoms the medication was controlling — a seizure, laboured breathing, collapse, or a diabetic dog who is weak, wobbly or disoriented.

Why doses get missed in households

Almost never because someone does not care. The pattern is nearly always the same: two or more people could have given it, each assumed the other had, and nobody checked. It is the same mechanism as double feeding, with a worse failure mode — and unlike food, you cannot tell by looking at the dog.

The classic triggers are a change in routine (a weekend, a trip, a visitor), a handover between people, and the second daily dose landing at a time when who is home varies.

A dosing routine that holds

  1. Attach it to something that already happens. With breakfast, with the evening walk. A time of day is a weaker anchor than an event.
  2. Use a pill organiser. The single most effective change available: you can see at a glance whether today's compartment is empty. No memory, no asking, no ambiguity. This also works when you are not home — someone can look.
  3. Name one owner per dose. Not “we give it in the morning”. One person owns the morning dose. A second person is the named backup.
  4. Keep medication off the children's rota entirely, at any age.
  5. Set a reminder that goes to the person on duty, not to everyone. A reminder everyone gets is a reminder nobody owns — and a shared reminder that four people dismiss is worse than none, because it creates the feeling of having handled it.
  6. Write down the missed-dose instruction for each drug when it is prescribed, while the vet is in front of you, and keep it with the medication. Then nobody is improvising at 10pm.
  7. Brief anyone who covers. Sitters, grandparents, dog walkers — exact dose, exact time, and what to do if it is missed. See our sitter instructions template.

Questions worth asking when a drug is prescribed

The FDA publishes a list of questions to ask your vet about any medication. The ones that matter most for a household with more than one person in it:

  • What exactly do I do if a dose is missed? (Ask for this drug specifically)
  • With food or without?
  • How critical is the exact timing — is an hour late fine, or not?
  • What side effects should make me call?
  • What happens if it is given twice by accident, and at what point is that an emergency?
  • Can it be given with the other things my dog takes?
  • How should it be stored, and does it need refrigerating?

Write the answers down. In six weeks nobody will remember what was said, and the person holding the bottle at the time may not be the person who was at the appointment.

Common questions

What happens if I miss a dose of my dog's medication?

For most medications, one missed dose is not a crisis. Give it if you remember within a few hours; skip it if you are more than halfway to the next. Never double up. Insulin, seizure medication and heartworm prevention are exceptions — call your vet for those.

Can I give two doses to catch up?

No. Doubling is where most harm from missed doses actually comes from, and with insulin or NSAIDs it can be serious. If you have already given a double dose by accident, call your vet now rather than waiting for symptoms.

How do I remember to give my dog medication?

A pill organiser plus one named person per dose covers most of it — the organiser makes it visible whether today's dose was given, which removes the asking entirely. Anchor the dose to an event rather than a clock time, and make sure reminders go to one person rather than to everybody.

My dog spat the tablet out — does that count as a missed dose?

Possibly, depending on how much was absorbed, which is a question for your vet rather than a guess. Mention that it happened. For next time, ask whether the tablet can be hidden in food, whether a liquid or flavoured version exists, and how to use a pill-giver properly.
