Dog vaccination schedule, puppy to adult

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Puppies get a core series starting at 6–8 weeks, boosted every 2–4 weeks until at least 16 weeks, then a booster at one year. After that, AAHA recommends DAPP every three years and rabies every one to three depending on local law. Non-core vaccines are generally annual. Leptospirosis moved into the core group for North American dogs in AAHA's 2024 update.

12 monthsBooster of everything in the puppy series

Why the series must run to at least 16 weeks. Puppies carry maternal antibodies that protect them early but also block vaccines from working. Those antibodies fade at an unpredictable age, somewhere between about 8 and 16 weeks. Repeating doses across that window is how you ensure at least one lands after the antibodies have gone. A puppy whose series stopped at 12 weeks may have no effective protection at all — which matters most for parvovirus, which kills.

Adult boosters: less often than most people think

After the one-year booster, AAHA recommends DAPP every three years rather than annually. Core vaccines produce immunological memory lasting at least three years, and annual boosting of these is explicitly described as not evidence-based for previously vaccinated adult dogs. Rabies follows local law — one or three years depending on jurisdiction and product.

Non-core vaccines are different: immunity is shorter, so leptospirosis, Bordetella, Lyme and canine influenza generally need annual boosting where they are indicated.

This does not mean skipping the annual visit. The examination is the valuable part — weight, teeth, heart, lumps, joints, parasite prevention — and most of what vets catch early is caught there rather than in the syringe.

Keeping the records somewhere you can find them

You will be asked for proof of vaccination by boarding kennels, daycare, groomers, training classes, some insurers, and anyone travelling with the dog. The paper card lives in a drawer and gets lost.

  • Photograph the vaccination card each time and keep it where more than one person can reach it
  • Note the due date, not just the given date
  • Record which product was used — rabies intervals differ by product
  • Keep flea, tick and worm treatment dates in the same place; these are the ones most commonly missed because they are monthly rather than annual
  • Make sure the person who takes the dog to the vet is not the only person who can find this

Normal reactions, and the ones that are not

Mild tiredness, a little soreness at the injection site, or being off food for a day are common and settle within 24–48 hours. Contact your vet promptly for swelling of the face or muzzle, hives, repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, difficulty breathing, or collapse — true allergic reactions are uncommon but appear quickly, usually within minutes to hours, and are treatable when caught.

Mention any previous reaction before the next vaccination. It changes what your vet does.

Common questions

Do dogs really need vaccines every year?

Not for the core vaccines. AAHA recommends DAPP every three years for previously vaccinated adults, and describes annual boosting of core vaccines as not evidence-based. Non-core vaccines such as leptospirosis and Bordetella do generally need annual boosting. The annual health check remains worthwhile regardless.

When can a puppy go outside after vaccination?

Ask your vet — it depends on the series and on local disease risk. There is a real trade-off: parvovirus risk on one side, and the closing socialisation window on the other. Most vets suggest carrying the puppy out, visiting clean private gardens, and attending well-run puppy classes that require vaccination, rather than total isolation until the series finishes.

What happens if my dog is overdue for a booster?

Usually your vet will restart or give a single booster depending on how overdue and which vaccine. It is rarely a crisis, but it does matter for boarding and travel paperwork, and lapsed rabies has legal implications in many places. Call and ask rather than waiting for the next annual visit.

Why did leptospirosis become a core vaccine?

AAHA moved it into the core group for North American dogs in its 2024 update, reflecting wider geographic spread and the fact that leptospirosis is a zoonosis — it can pass to people. Recommendations outside North America vary, so ask your own vet.
