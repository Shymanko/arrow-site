The family dog chore chart, done properly

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

A dog chore chart works when it does two things: puts a name in every slot rather than a tick box, and rotates the unpopular slots. Keep it to eight or ten rows — longer charts stop being read by week two. Our free chart maker builds and prints one in about a minute.

What goes on the chart

Start with the six that genuinely have to happen every day, then add only what applies to your dog. Resist the urge to be comprehensive; a chart nobody reads is worse than no chart, because it creates the impression the problem is handled.

The two design rules that decide whether it survives

1. Assign, don’t just list

A chart with chores down one side and empty tick boxes across the top looks organised and changes nothing. An unassigned job belongs to everyone, which means it belongs to no one, and in practice it lands on whoever has the lowest tolerance for it being undone — usually the same person who made the chart. Put a name in every single box.

2. Rotate the slots nobody wants

Fixed assignments feel efficient and quietly calcify. One person always takes the 7am walk because they are up anyway; six months later that is simply their life, and the fact that the minute counts are roughly equal does not make it feel fair. Rotating by day spreads the early mornings, the rain and the yard clean-up. Our generator does this automatically, shifting each job by one person each day.

Keep medication off children's rows, always. Every other job on the chart has a forgiving failure mode. A missed or doubled dose does not, and the responsibility for it should sit with a named adult every day of the week.

Making it work for children

Children can take real responsibility for a dog, as long as the job matches the age — and as long as everyone understands that an adult remains accountable for the animal's welfare regardless of what the chart says. Broadly: under-fives help rather than do; 5–8 can measure food, brush and hold a lead alongside an adult; 9–12 can feed with a reminder and walk in a safe familiar area; teenagers can own feeding and walking outright. Our guide to dog chores for kids by age goes through it properly.

One thing that makes a visible difference: let the child tick their own box. The ticking is most of the motivation, and an adult who ticks it for them has removed the entire point.

Where paper charts fail

Fridge charts are genuinely good at one thing and genuinely bad at another.

They are good at making invisible work visible. A household that has never written the jobs down usually discovers two things in week one: there are more of them than anyone thought, and the distribution is less even than anyone thought. That discovery alone is worth the exercise, and it tends to end the argument about whether one person is doing more.

They are bad at telling you what has actually happened, because you have to be standing in the kitchen to read one. The failure is familiar: you are at work, you text the group chat “has anyone walked her?”, and you get back two questions and a thumbs up of uncertain meaning.

A chart is a rota. The hard part is the record

Whatever you use — paper, whiteboard, shared note, app — the thing that actually reduces friction is a record of what has been done that everybody can see from wherever they are. The rota tells people whose turn it is. The record stops them having to ask.

Common questions

What chores should be on a dog chore chart?

At minimum: morning feed, evening feed, fresh water, morning walk, evening walk and the last trip out before bed. Add medication, yard clean-up, brushing, training and bowl-washing as they apply. Keep it under about ten rows.

How do I make a dog chore chart?

Use our free chart maker: add everyone's names, tick the jobs, choose whether to rotate, and print. Or draw it by hand — jobs down the left, seven days across the top, a name and a tick box in every cell.

Do chore charts actually work for pet care?

They work well for making the workload visible and settling who owns what. They work poorly as a live record, because reading one requires being in the room. Most households find the chart solves the fairness argument and something shared on phones solves the “has anyone fed her?” one.
