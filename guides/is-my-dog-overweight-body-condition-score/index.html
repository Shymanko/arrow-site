Is my dog overweight? A 30-second check

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Use the vets' own method, which takes half a minute and needs no scale. Feel the ribs — they should be easy to find under a thin layer, like the back of your knuckles. Look from above — there should be a visible waist behind the ribs. Look from the side — the belly should tuck up toward the hind legs. Fail any two and your dog is probably carrying extra weight.

Look, feel, look

Body condition scoring is the standard clinical tool for this, and the WSAVA protocol is deliberately hands-on, because fur hides a great deal. The scale runs 1 to 9, where 4–5 is ideal and each point above 5 represents roughly 10–15% over ideal weight.

1. Feel the ribs

Run your hands flat along both sides of the chest with light pressure. A useful calibration: with your palm down, feel the knuckles on the back of your own hand. That is roughly what an ideal dog's ribs should feel like — discernible under a thin covering, not sharp, not buried. If you have to press to find them, there is too much over them. If they feel like your knuckles with your hand in a fist, too little.

2. Look from above

Stand over your standing dog. You are looking for an hourglass: the body should narrow noticeably behind the ribs and before the hips. A straight outline from chest to hips means overweight; a bulging one, more so.

3. Look from the side

Crouch to the dog's level. The underline should rise from the bottom of the ribcage up toward the hind legs — the abdominal tuck. A belly that runs level, or hangs lower than the ribcage, indicates excess weight.

The 9-point scale adopted by WSAVA. Scores 4–5 are ideal.
ScoreWhat you see and feel Meaning
1–3Ribs, spine and hip bones visible from a distance; no fat you can feel; obvious loss of muscleUnderweight — see a vet
4–5Ribs easily felt with minimal fat over them; waist visible from above; clear tuck from the sideIdeal (~15–24% body fat)
6–7Ribs take pressure to find; waist barely visible or absent; tuck reduced; fat pads at the base of the tailOverweight (~25–34% body fat)
8–9Ribs not palpable under heavy fat; no waist; abdomen distended; fat deposits on the lower back, limbs and neckObese (35%+ body fat)

Coat changes the look, not the facts. Double-coated and long-haired breeds can hide two body condition points. This is why the hands matter more than the eyes — do the rib check on a Collie or a Husky before trusting what you see.

Almost nobody thinks it is their dog

The Association for Pet Obesity Prevention's US survey found 59% of dogs overweight or obese — 37% overweight, 22% obese. In the same research, only 17% of dog owners described their own dog as overweight, and 84% rated their dog's body condition as healthy.

That gap is not owners being careless. It is that we calibrate against the dogs we see, and if six out of ten of those are carrying extra weight, a dog at an ideal score starts to look thin. Several breed-specific versions of this exist too — a fit Labrador looks skinny to people used to Labradors.

Why it matters more than it looks

Excess weight raises the risk of osteoarthritis, diabetes, heart disease, kidney and liver disease, and several cancers, and makes anaesthesia riskier. The clearest single piece of evidence is Purina's 14-year study of 48 Labradors: littermates fed 25% less lived a median of 13.0 years against 11.2, and did not need treatment for age-related chronic conditions until 12.0 years against 9.9. Nearly two extra years, and two extra years of comfort, from portion size alone.

If the answer is yes

  1. Start at the vet, not with the food bag. Weight gain can be a symptom — hypothyroidism and Cushing's both cause it — and a target weight should be set by someone who has examined the dog.
  2. Weight loss is calculated from the target weight, not today's weight. This is the part people get wrong at home, and getting it wrong means either no progress or dangerously fast loss. Safe loss is roughly 1–2% of body weight per week.
  3. Audit the treats before you cut the meals. In most households there are more calories in the uncounted extras than anyone expects, and cutting meals while leaving treats untouched makes the nutritional balance worse.
  4. Weigh the food. Scooping drifts; a scale does not.
  5. Add movement gradually. An unfit, overweight dog should not start with long runs. Build up, and be cautious in heat — overweight dogs overheat faster.
  6. Re-check monthly with the same hands-on method, and get everyone in the house to do the rib check so it is not one person's opinion.

Common questions

What is a good body condition score for a dog?

4 or 5 on the 9-point WSAVA scale. That means ribs easily felt under a thin fat layer, a visible waist from above, and an abdominal tuck from the side.

How can I tell if my dog is overweight without scales?

That is exactly what body condition scoring is for. The three checks above need no equipment and are more informative than a number on a scale, because they measure body composition rather than mass. Scales are for tracking change over time once you know where you are starting.

How fast should a dog lose weight?

Roughly 1–2% of body weight per week, under veterinary supervision. Faster loss carries its own risks and is more likely to rebound. For most dogs this means a programme measured in months.

My dog looks fine but the vet says overweight — who is right?

Probably the vet, and not because you are not paying attention. Clinicians assess hundreds of dogs and calibrate against the ideal; owners calibrate against the dogs around them, and most of those are heavy. Ask them to show you the rib check on your own dog so you can repeat it at home.
