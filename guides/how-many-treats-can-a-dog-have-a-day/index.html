How many treats can a dog have a day?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 6 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Treats, chews, training rewards and table scraps should together stay under 10% of your dog's daily calories — and that 10% comes out of the daily total, not on top of it. For a 20 kg dog on about 1,060 kcal, that is roughly 105 kcal of treats a day. A single large dental chew can use most of it.

What the 10% rule actually means

Three details that are usually left out:

  • It is 10% of calories, not 10% of volume or ten treats. A handful of training kibble and one large biscuit can be the same number of calories.
  • It includes everything. Dental chews, bully sticks, the crust from someone's toast, the cheese used to hide a tablet, the biscuit the neighbour hands over at the gate.
  • The remaining 90% must come from complete food. That is the nutritional reason for the rule, not just the calorie one: complete diets are formulated so every vitamin, mineral and amino acid lands in the right ratio. Treats are not. The more of the day's calories that come from treats, the more diluted the overall nutrition becomes.

The practical takeaway: a dog can have a dozen small training treats a day without trouble, or one dental chew, but not both. And a daily dental chew for a small dog can be a sixth of everything they eat.

Low-calorie alternatives that still feel like treats

  • Carrot sticks, cucumber, green beans, plain cooked pumpkin, small pieces of apple (no seeds), blueberries
  • Kibble taken from the measured daily ration — most dogs are perfectly happy to work for their own food, and it costs nothing extra
  • Air-dried single-ingredient treats broken into much smaller pieces: a dog values the event of getting a treat far more than the size of it

Never: grapes, raisins, onion, garlic, macadamia nuts, chocolate, or anything containing xylitol (birch sugar) — found in some peanut butters, and acutely dangerous. Check the label of any peanut butter before it goes in a toy. If your dog eats any of these, call your vet or a pet poison line immediately rather than waiting for symptoms.

The four-people problem

This is where the 10% rule quietly dies. Each person gives the dog one or two treats a day. Individually that is nothing. Collectively it is six to eight treats, which for a small dog can be half their daily calories — and nobody has done anything they would describe as overfeeding.

It gets worse with visiting family. Grandparents in particular tend to express affection through food, and a weekend visit can add several hundred unlogged calories.

Two fixes that work better than asking people to use restraint:

  1. Put the day's treat allowance in a jar each morning. When the jar is empty, the treats are done. Nobody has to count, and nobody has to be the enforcer.
  2. Redirect the affection rather than banning it. The impulse behind a treat is love, not calories. Five minutes of fetch, a brush, a short training session or a walk all scratch exactly the same itch and cost nothing. This matters: dogs who stay lean live a median of 1.8 years longer, by Purina's 14-year Labrador study.

Common questions

Is one treat a day too much for a dog?

It depends entirely on the treat and the dog. One small training treat is negligible for any dog. One large dental chew is most of a 20 kg dog's entire daily allowance and more than a 5 kg dog's.

Do dental chews count as treats?

Yes. They are often among the most calorific things a dog eats in a day. If you use one daily for dental health — a reasonable thing to do — reduce the meals accordingly and skip other treats that day.

Can I use my dog's own food as treats?

Yes, and it is the single easiest fix. Set aside a portion of the measured daily ration in a pouch and use it for training. It is nutritionally complete, it costs nothing extra, and it cannot break the 10% rule because it is part of the 90%.

What if my dog only trains for high-value treats?

Use them, in much smaller pieces. A pea-sized piece of chicken works as well as a cube for most dogs, and budget for it by trimming the meal. High-value rewards are worth it for genuinely hard training — recall, vet handling — rather than spent on sitting in the kitchen.
