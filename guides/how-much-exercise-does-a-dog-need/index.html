How much exercise does a dog need?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Most adult dogs need 30 minutes to 2 hours a day, split across at least two outings. High-energy working breeds sit at the top of that range; flat-faced and low-energy breeds at the bottom. Puppies follow the five-minute rule — roughly five minutes of formal exercise per month of age, up to twice a day.

Two common surprises. Greyhounds are sprinters, not endurance athletes, and are famously content with a couple of short walks and a long sleep. Flat-faced breeds should be exercised with real caution in warmth — a shortened airway makes panting far less effective at cooling, and they overheat at temperatures other dogs handle easily.

Puppies: the five-minute rule

Roughly five minutes of formal exercise per month of age, up to twice a day. A four-month-old gets about 20 minutes twice daily. The rule applies until physical maturity — around 12 months for small breeds, 18 months or more for large ones.

The reason is growth plates. Repetitive high-impact activity in a growing dog — long runs, jumping down from heights, stairs repeatedly, forced road walking — is associated with joint problems later, and large breeds are most at risk because they grow for longest.

Free play is not the same as formal exercise. A puppy pottering, sniffing and playing in the garden at their own pace, stopping when tired, is fine and does not count against the five-minute budget. The rule is about sustained, human-paced activity, where the puppy keeps going because you are still going.

Sniffing counts more than distance

A dog on a 20-minute walk allowed to stop and sniff is often more satisfied than one marched briskly for 45 minutes. Scent work is genuinely demanding mental activity, and mental work tires dogs out in a way that pure distance does not.

This matters practically. On days when a long walk is impossible — heat, rain, a twelve-hour shift, a sick child — a 15-minute “sniffari” where the dog chooses the route, or ten minutes of scattering their dinner in the grass, or five minutes of training, buys back a surprising amount of the deficit.

Mental work that counts

  • Sniffari walks: the dog picks the pace and direction
  • Scatter feeding in grass or a snuffle mat
  • Puzzle feeders and frozen stuffed toys
  • Five minutes of training — new tricks are more tiring than known ones
  • “Find it” games with hidden treats around the house

Too much, and too little

Under-exercised dogs show it through behaviour: destructiveness, pacing, barking at nothing, inability to settle, weight gain, attention-seeking. Most behaviour complaints in young dogs resolve substantially with more physical and mental work.

Over-exercised dogs are less often recognised. Watch for stiffness or limping after exercise or the next morning, reluctance to set off, excessive panting, sore or cracked pads, and — counter-intuitively — being more wired rather than less, which can indicate a dog running on adrenaline rather than genuinely tired. Puppies, seniors, heavy dogs and flat-faced breeds are the ones most likely to be pushed too hard.

Seniors keep walking, differently

Older dogs still need daily exercise; what changes is intensity and duration. Shorter, more frequent outings beat one long one. Keep it up — dogs who stop walking lose muscle, which makes arthritis worse, which makes walking harder. Any new reluctance to walk, limping, or difficulty on stairs is a vet conversation, because canine arthritis is both very common and very treatable, and “slowing down with age” is frequently untreated pain.

Dividing the walks across a household

Two walks a day is the standard answer, and in a shared household it is also where the workload quietly becomes uneven. The morning walk is harder than the evening one, and whoever is up anyway ends up doing it every day without anyone deciding that. Rotating the morning slot costs nothing and prevents a specific, common resentment — see splitting dog care with your partner.

Common questions

Is one walk a day enough for a dog?

For most dogs, no. Two outings is the usual minimum — partly for exercise and partly because a healthy adult dog needs to go out every four to six hours anyway. A single daily walk tends to mean long gaps either side of it.

How long should I walk my puppy?

About five minutes per month of age, up to twice a day — 15 minutes twice daily at three months. Free play at the puppy's own pace is additional and fine.

Does playing in the yard count as exercise?

Partly. It covers movement but not the mental stimulation of new smells and environments, which is a large part of what a walk provides. Most dogs in a familiar garden potter rather than exercise. It is a supplement, not a replacement.

My dog still has energy after a long walk — what now?

Add mental work rather than distance. Many high-energy breeds can be walked into better fitness rather than tiredness, so you end up with an athlete who needs more every week. Training, scent games and puzzle feeding tire these dogs far more efficiently than another kilometre.
