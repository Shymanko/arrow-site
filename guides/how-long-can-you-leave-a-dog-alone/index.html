How long can you leave a dog alone?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

The RSPCA advises leaving an adult dog alone for no more than four hours at a stretch. Puppies need far less: a few minutes at under ten weeks, building to one or two hours by five months. Seniors drop back to two to four. Longer than four hours is not automatically cruel, but it does mean arranging a midday break — and the limit is about toileting, boredom and distress together, not bladder capacity alone.

Where the four-hour figure comes from

It is welfare guidance, not a bladder measurement. The RSPCA's position is that a dog should not go longer than four hours without the opportunity to exercise, go to the toilet and be with people. All three parts matter. A dog can physically hold on for eight hours and still be suffering at hour six from sheer boredom and isolation — dogs are a social species and being alone is itself the hard part.

When the workday is eight hours

Most people asking this question are not deciding whether to get a dog — they already have one and a job. The realistic options, roughly in order of how well they work:

  • Split the day. A midday visit from a walker, a neighbour or a family member turns one eight-hour block into two four-hour ones. This is the single highest-value change available.
  • Stagger the household. If two people work different shifts, overlapping departures badly is a scheduling accident, not a fact of life. Compare calendars properly.
  • Daycare two or three days a week. Expensive, and not right for every dog — anxious or reactive dogs often find it worse than being home.
  • Make the alone time less empty. A long walk before you leave, a frozen stuffed toy, a snuffle mat, a view of the street. These help with boredom. They do not help with toileting or with genuine separation anxiety.

How to tell it is actually going badly

A dog that sleeps for seven of the eight hours is in a very different situation from one that spends them distressed. Look for the evidence:

  • Destruction specifically at exit points — door frames, window sills, the letterbox
  • Accidents indoors from a house-trained dog
  • Reports of howling or barking from neighbours
  • Drooling, scratched paws or a wet chest when you get home
  • Escalating anxiety as you get ready to leave, rather than at the door
  • A dog who cannot settle for the first half hour after you return

That cluster is separation anxiety, and it is a genuine welfare problem that does not improve on its own. It is also treatable — with a structured desensitisation plan from a qualified behaviourist, sometimes alongside medication from your vet. What does not work is tiring the dog out more, getting a second dog, or waiting for them to grow out of it.

Building tolerance starts on day one. The evidence is consistent: dogs gradually introduced to being alone when they first arrive are substantially less likely to develop problems later. Start with seconds, not hours — step outside, come back before any distress begins, and build from there. Counter-intuitively, the puppies who are never left alone in the first months are the ones most likely to struggle later.

Two people, one dog, four assumptions

There is a specific failure mode in shared households: both adults leave in the morning, each assuming the other arranged the midday break, and the dog does nine hours. It happens most often on days that deviate from the normal pattern — a school run swapped, an early meeting, a dentist appointment.

The fix is to make the midday break a named job on a specific day with a specific person, reviewed when the week changes, rather than a standing understanding that quietly stops holding the first time someone's schedule moves.

Common questions

Is it cruel to leave a dog alone for 8 hours?

It is longer than the RSPCA's four-hour guidance, so it needs mitigating rather than normalising — most commonly by splitting the day with a midday visit. A dog doing eight hours alone five days a week with no break, no enrichment and no toilet opportunity is a welfare concern.

Can a dog be left alone overnight?

No. Even a dog comfortable alone during the day should not be left overnight without a person: there is no toilet opportunity, no response if something goes wrong, and a long unbroken stretch of isolation. Use a sitter, and leave them proper instructions.

Does a second dog help with being alone?

Sometimes with boredom. Rarely with separation anxiety, which is usually about the absence of a specific person rather than loneliness in general. Getting a second dog to fix the first one's anxiety frequently produces two anxious dogs.
