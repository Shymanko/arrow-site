How often should my dog go out?

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

A healthy adult dog needs to go out every 4 to 6 hours during the day — three to five times — and can usually manage 8 hours overnight. Puppies need roughly one break per month of age: a three-month-old every three hours or so. Seniors drop back to around every four hours. Illness, medication, heat and anxiety all shorten every one of those numbers.

Why one hour per month works for puppies

The rule trainers and vets both quote — a puppy can hold its bladder about one hour for each month of age — is a rough approximation of bladder capacity and sphincter control developing together. A four-month-old can manage roughly four hours, give or take one.

Two things people get wrong about it. First, it describes a maximum, not a schedule: if you take a puppy out only at the limit, you are training them to hold on until desperate, which is the opposite of what you want. Second, it stops being meaningful after about eight months, because the constraint is no longer physical.

Puppies also tend to go within about fifteen minutes of eating or drinking, which is why the single most effective change most people can make is a trip outside after every meal, nap and play session — not a shorter clock interval.

What makes the interval shorter

  • Medication. Diuretics and steroids both dramatically increase urine output. A dog on furosemide is not on a normal schedule and never will be.
  • Heat and exercise. More water in, more water out. A hot July afternoon can turn a six-hour dog into a four-hour one.
  • Diet. Wet food carries far more water than kibble. Switching from one to the other will visibly change frequency within a day or two.
  • A new environment. Newly adopted dogs, and dogs staying with family, often need extra trips for a week or two. Stress and an unfamiliar surface both play a part.
  • Diagnosed conditions. Kidney disease, diabetes, Cushing's and urinary incontinence all raise output. Your vet's interval beats any guide's.

How to tell your dog needs to go, before the puddle

Dogs signal well before they are desperate; the signals are just easy to miss while you are doing something else. Watch for circling or sniffing in a tight pattern, a sudden departure toward the door or a rug, restlessness after settling down, whining at nobody in particular, or scratching at the door.

What very few dogs do is ask clearly. If yours is subtle, teach a bell on the door handle — ring it every single time you open the door for a toilet trip, and within a couple of weeks most dogs start ringing it themselves.

When frequency is a medical sign, not a scheduling problem

Call your vet rather than adjusting the rota if you see: straining or crying while urinating; blood, or dark or cloudy urine; a house-trained dog suddenly having accidents indoors; a marked increase in drinking alongside the urinating; or a puppy over four months who still cannot last an hour. Any of these can be a urinary tract infection, bladder stones, or an early sign of kidney disease or diabetes — all far more treatable caught early.

The coordination problem nobody writes about

Every guide on this topic ends with an interval, as if the hard part were knowing the number. In a household with three or four people, the hard part is knowing when the dog last went out. “She went out a while ago” routinely means two hours to the person saying it and five to the clock.

So before you optimise the interval, fix the record. A whiteboard by the door works. A shared note works. What does not work is four people each holding a partial picture and assuming theirs is the complete one.

Common questions

How many times a day should a dog pee?

Three to five times a day for a healthy adult. Consistently fewer than three, or suddenly more than six or seven, is worth raising with your vet.

Should I take my dog out at night?

Adults and most dogs over six months sleep through. Puppies under four months usually need at least one night trip — set an alarm rather than waiting for them to wake you, so you keep the trip calm and boring.

Is twice a day enough for a dog to go out?

No. Twice a day means roughly 12-hour gaps, which is beyond the documented upper limit even for a healthy adult. Three to five trips is the normal range, and at least one of them should be a proper walk rather than a 60-second garden trip.
