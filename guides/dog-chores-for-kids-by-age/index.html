Dog chores for kids, by age

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Under 5: helping, not doing — tidying toys, holding your hand over theirs on the brush. 5–8: measuring food, brushing, holding the lead alongside an adult. 9–12: feeding with a reminder, walking in a safe familiar area. 13+: owning feeding and walking on known routes. Medication, traffic and any reactive dog stay with an adult at every age.

The honest framing first

Getting a dog “to teach the children responsibility” mostly does not work the way people hope, and it is worth saying so before the chore chart goes up. Children's enthusiasm for pet care is real and also seasonal. An adult has to be the backstop every single day, because the dog does not get a day off while a nine-year-old learns about commitment.

What does work: give children jobs they can genuinely succeed at, let them own those jobs visibly, and keep the ones with serious consequences away from them. Children who succeed at small pet-care jobs take on bigger ones willingly. Children set up to fail at jobs beyond them learn that pet care is nagging.

Ages 2–4: helping, with you

At this age the child is learning by being near the care, not performing it. Children under about five should not be solely responsible for any pet-care task.

  • Putting dog toys away in a basket — genuinely useful and very popular
  • Drying the bowl after you have washed it
  • Brushing with your hand over theirs
  • Helping carry the (light, part-filled) water bowl
  • Learning how to touch a dog: open hand, soft strokes on the shoulder or chest, away from ears, eyes, mouth and tail

The most valuable thing a toddler can learn here is not a chore at all. It is leaving the dog alone when the dog walks away — the foundation of every safe interaction later.

Ages 5–8: real jobs, supervised

  • Measuring and pouring food into the bowl — with an adult present, because dogs get excitable around food
  • Brushing properly
  • Refilling the water bowl
  • Tidying and organising leads, toys and supplies
  • Holding the lead alongside an adult who also has hold of it
  • Ticking their own boxes on the chore chart — this is most of the motivation

Feeding is a supervised job at this age, not a solo one. A dog that is normally gentle can be sharp around a food bowl, and a child bending over the bowl is at face height. Adult in the room, every time.

Ages 9–12: independence with a safety net

  • Feeding independently — expect to give reminders, and verify rather than assume
  • Walking in a safe, familiar, low-traffic area, once you have watched them handle the dog several times
  • Helping with baths
  • Yard clean-up
  • Learning to spot that something is off: not eating, limping, unusual lethargy. This is a genuinely valuable skill and children are often better observers than adults

The judgement call on walking is about the dog as much as the child. A 35 kg dog who pulls is not a job for a 10-year-old regardless of how responsible they are, because the failure mode is a loose dog near a road.

Ages 13+: ownership

  • Full responsibility for feeding, including measuring correctly
  • Walking alone on familiar routes
  • Keeping water topped up and bowls clean
  • Yard clean-up
  • Being a competent second pair of hands at the vet

Teenagers can genuinely carry a share of the load. What they cannot carry is the accountability — exams, social lives and moods all legitimately take precedence for a teenager in a way they cannot for the dog's dinner.

Jobs that stay with an adult at every age

  • Medication. No exceptions. A missed or doubled dose is a different category of mistake from a missed brush
  • Walking near traffic, and walking any dog strong enough to pull a child over
  • Handling a reactive, fearful or unwell dog
  • Introducing the dog to strange dogs or strangers
  • Anything involving a dog who is eating, sleeping or in pain — the three situations where ordinarily tolerant dogs are least tolerant

Making the chart match the ages

When you build a family chore chart, give children their own rows rather than putting them on the same rotation as adults. It makes the age boundary visible, it stops the 7am walk landing on a nine-year-old by rotation, and it lets a child see their own column fill up — which, for most children under about eleven, is the entire reward structure.

Common questions

What age can a child walk a dog alone?

Around 9–12 for a familiar, well-behaved dog on a quiet route, after you have watched them handle it several times. The deciding factor is usually the dog: any dog strong enough to pull the child over should not be walked by them, at any age.

Should kids be responsible for feeding the dog?

From about nine, with reminders and verification. From five to eight they can measure and pour with an adult present. The adult stays accountable for the dog actually having eaten — which is why a shared log beats trusting a report.

How do I get my kids to stick with dog chores?

Small jobs they can finish, a visible chart, and letting them tick their own boxes. Stop treating lapses as character failures — remind, do it together a few times, and keep the job small enough to succeed at. And accept that adults are the backstop, because the dog cannot wait for a lesson in responsibility to land.
