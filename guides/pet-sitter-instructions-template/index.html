Pet sitter instructions: what to actually leave

Updated 2026-10-26 · 7 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

The non-negotiables: your contact details, your vet's number and address, an out-of-hours emergency vet, feeding amounts and times, medication with exact doses, and written permission to seek treatment. Everything else is helpful; those six are what matters at 11pm when something is wrong. Copy the template below.

The template

Copy this, fill it in, and leave a printed copy on the counter as well as sending it — phones go flat and Wi-Fi passwords live on paper.

1. Emergency contacts — top of the page, always

  • Your mobile, and a second number you can be reached on
  • Where you are staying, and the time zone
  • A local backup contact who has a key and can get to the house
  • Your vet: practice name, address, phone, your dog's registered name
  • Out-of-hours emergency vet: often a different practice. Look it up before you leave and write the address down
  • Pet insurance policy number and insurer phone
  • Written authorisation: “I authorise [sitter] to seek veterinary treatment for [dog] in my absence, up to [amount], and will be responsible for the cost.” Signed and dated. Many practices will ask

2. About the dog

  • Name, breed, age, weight, and a current photo — the photo matters if they go missing
  • Microchip number and the registration database
  • Spayed or neutered
  • Allergies — food, medication, environmental
  • Existing conditions and anything to watch for
  • Vaccination status and when flea, tick and worm treatment were last given

3. Feeding — be specific

  • Exact food, in grams or cups, at exact times. Not “a scoop” — show them the scoop or leave a measured container
  • Where the food is kept and how much is there
  • Permitted treats and the daily limit
  • Things the dog must never have, including anything in your own kitchen
  • Whether the dog resource-guards food or bowls
  • Whether they are fed before or after a walk, and how long to wait between

4. Medication — the part worth over-explaining

For every medication: name, dose, exact time, with or without food, how it is given, where it is kept, and what to do if a dose is missed. Pre-sort doses into a labelled pill organiser so there is no counting under pressure. Ask your vet in advance what their missed-dose instruction is for each drug, and write that down — never leave a sitter to guess, and make it explicit that they should never double up to catch up.

5. The routine, hour by hour

  • Wake and bedtime
  • Walk times, how long, and where — plus where not to go
  • Toilet break times, including the last one before bed
  • Where the dog sleeps, and whether they are allowed on furniture
  • How long they are used to being alone, and what that looks like
  • Favourite games and toys; what settles them when they are anxious

6. Behaviour — the honest version

This is where owners soften things and it helps nobody. Write down what is actually true: pulls hard on the lead; reactive to other dogs on lead; bolts through open doors; takes food from the counter; afraid of fireworks or bins; does not like being touched while asleep; recall is unreliable off lead. A sitter who knows can manage it. A sitter who finds out is dealing with an incident.

7. House and access

  • Keys or door code, alarm code and procedure, parking
  • Wi-Fi name and password, written out
  • Where leads, waste bags, towels, cleaning supplies and spare bedding live
  • Bin day
  • Stopcock and fuse box, in case
  • Whether photos of your dog may be posted publicly — say either way

What owners forget

  • The out-of-hours vet, which is often not the usual practice
  • Written authorisation to treat, and a cost ceiling
  • Enough food for the whole stay plus a few days in case you are delayed
  • What to do about a missed medication dose
  • How the dog behaves when left alone in this house
  • That the lead clip is faulty, or the back gate does not latch properly
  • A photo clear enough to use on a lost poster
  • Telling your vet that someone else may bring the dog in

Handovers work better in person

If you can, walk the sitter through one feed and one walk before you go. Fifteen minutes of showing removes more confusion than three pages of writing, particularly around the things that are hard to describe — how hard the dog pulls, how the back door lock sticks, what the dog does when they want to go out.

And leave a way for them to tell you what happened that is not a stream of texts you read at midnight in another time zone. A shared checklist they can tick off means you can look once and see that everything happened, without either of you managing the other.

Common questions

What should I leave for my pet sitter?

Your contact details, your vet and an out-of-hours emergency vet, written authorisation to seek treatment, exact feeding amounts and times, medication with doses and missed-dose instructions, the daily routine, honest behaviour notes, and house access details. Plus food for the whole stay and a few days extra.

Should I pay a friend or neighbour to pet sit?

Offering is good practice even if they decline — it converts a favour into an arrangement, which makes it far easier to be specific about what you need and to ask again. Either way, give them the same written instructions you would give a professional.

How do I give a sitter access without adding them to everything?

Keep it time-limited and scoped: a printed sheet and a key, or a guest link that expires. The thing to avoid is adding someone to a permanent family group chat for a four-day trip, which is awkward to undo and means they see everything.

What if my dog needs medication while I'm away?

Pre-sort doses into a labelled organiser, write the exact schedule down, and ask your vet in advance what to do about a missed dose for that specific drug — the answer differs by medication. Make it explicit that the sitter should never double up to catch up, and should call the practice if in doubt.
