A dog care schedule that survives a full-time job

Updated 2026-10-26 · 8 min read · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

A dog can live well with full-time workers, but the midday gap has to be covered by someone — a walker, a neighbour, a family member or a staggered shift — because the RSPCA's guidance is a maximum of four hours alone. Front-load the morning with a real walk, not a rushed one, and decide the cover plan weekly rather than each morning.

The constraint you are working around

Four hours is the RSPCA's maximum for an adult dog alone, covering toileting, exercise and company together. Most full-time schedules produce a gap of eight or nine. The entire problem is that gap, and nearly every workable schedule solves it the same way: split it.

Three worked timetables

One person, 9–5, commuting

TimeWhat happens
06:45Out of bed. Straight outside for a toilet trip
07:00Proper walk, 30–45 minutes. This is the one that has to be real — it carries the whole day
07:50Breakfast, then out again briefly
08:15Leave. Frozen stuffed toy or puzzle feeder as you go
12:30Cover: walker, neighbour or family. 20–30 minutes, toilet and a bit of company
17:45Home. Outside immediately, before anything else
18:30Second walk, 30 minutes. Sniffing counts more than distance
19:00Dinner, then out
20:00Five minutes of training or a puzzle. Mental work tires dogs out
22:30Last call

The morning walk is the load-bearing element. A dog who has had 40 minutes of proper exercise and sniffing before 8am settles for the day in a way that a dog given ten rushed minutes does not.

Two people, both working, no walker

The highest-value move here is almost never hiring someone — it is comparing calendars properly. Most two-worker households have more flexibility than they are using:

  • Stagger the departures. One leaves at 07:30 and is home at 16:30; the other leaves at 09:30 and is home at 18:30. The same total hours produce a gap of about six instead of nine, and the morning walk stops being a race.
  • Split the morning. The early leaver does the first toilet trip; the later one does the proper walk after the rush.
  • Use the work-from-home day deliberately. If either of you has one, that is the day for the vet, the long walk and the grooming — not an ordinary day that happens to be at home.
  • Lunch at home, even once a week, if one of you works close enough.

Shift work or irregular hours

Fixed timetables do not survive shift work; the thing that does is a weekly plan agreed in advance. Sit down once a week with both rotas, mark the gaps, and assign cover for each one before the week starts. Dogs adapt well to varying times and badly to varying amounts — it is the dropped walk, not the late one, that causes problems.

Watch for the day that breaks the pattern. The dog rarely suffers on ordinary days. It happens on the day someone has an early meeting, or the school run swaps, or a dentist appointment lands at noon — and both people assume the other adjusted. Those days need an explicit handover, not an assumption.

What to leave the dog with

Enrichment does not replace a toilet break or company, but it does change how the hours feel. A frozen stuffed toy given as you leave turns departure into something good. A snuffle mat scattered with part of the daily ration gives twenty minutes of work. A view of the street suits some dogs and winds others up — watch which yours is. Leave something that smells of you.

If you come home to destruction at exit points, accidents from a house-trained dog, or reports of howling, that is separation distress rather than boredom, and more enrichment will not fix it — see how long you can leave a dog alone.

Plan the week, not the morning

Almost every household that struggles with this is deciding each day at 7am who is doing what. That works until the first schedule change, which happens most weeks. Ten minutes on a Sunday assigning the week's slots — and particularly the midday cover for each day — removes most of the daily friction, and makes the days that need special handling visible before they arrive rather than after.

Common questions

Can you have a dog if you work 9 to 5?

Yes, provided the midday gap is covered — a walker, a neighbour, family, or staggered working hours. What does not work is a dog left nine hours alone five days a week with no break, which exceeds welfare guidance on every measure.

How long can a dog be left alone while I work?

The RSPCA advises a maximum of four hours at a time for an adult dog. Puppies and seniors need considerably less.

Is a dog walker worth it?

For a household where everyone works full time, it is usually the single highest-value spend in dog ownership — it converts one nine-hour gap into two four-hour ones. Check insurance, ask whether walks are solo or group, and give them written instructions.

What if my dog is alone more than four hours occasionally?

An occasional longer day for a healthy adult dog who is comfortable alone is not a crisis. The concern is the standing pattern. If long days are becoming the norm rather than the exception, that is the point to arrange cover.
