How often should my dog go out?

Updated 2026-10-26 · Takes 10 seconds · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Most healthy adult dogs need to go out every 4 to 6 hours during the day — three to five trips — and can manage 8 hours overnight. Puppies need it far more often: roughly one hour of bladder control per month of age. Seniors usually move back toward the puppy end. What the calculator is actually doing

There is no single “correct” number of walks a day, but there is a well-established range, and almost every reputable veterinary source lands in the same place. The calculator applies three rules.

1. For puppies: one hour per month of age

This is the rule of thumb used by trainers and vets alike. A three-month-old puppy can hold it for about three hours; a five-month-old for about five. It stops being useful past about eight months, because by then the limit is no longer physical.

Crucially, that is the maximum, not the target. We schedule breaks slightly more often than the hold time, because a puppy who is taken out before they are desperate learns far faster than one who is rushed outside mid-accident.

2. For adults: every 4–6 hours in the day, up to 8 overnight

Healthy adult dogs urinate three to five times a day. The night figure is higher than the day figure for a physiological reason, not a convenience one: the body slows urine production during sleep, and a sleeping dog is neither drinking nor exercising. Ten to twelve hours is the documented upper limit — a ceiling to avoid, not a schedule to aim for.

3. For seniors: shorter, and watch for change

Bladder muscle tone declines with age and age-related conditions become more likely, so the usable window shrinks to roughly four to six hours in the day and five to six overnight. The more important signal for an older dog is not the absolute number but the direction of travel: a dog who needed four trips a day last year and needs seven now should see a vet.

Size matters less than people expect. Large dogs do have larger bladders relative to body size and can generally hold on longer, but breed-by-breed differences are smaller than the differences between a puppy, an adult and a senior. Age first, then size.

When the answer is “call your vet”, not “adjust the schedule”

No calculator can tell the difference between a dog who needs a shorter interval and a dog who is ill. Treat these as medical, not logistical:

  • Straining, crying, or producing only drops
  • Blood, or urine that has turned dark or cloudy
  • A reliably house-trained dog who starts having accidents indoors
  • A noticeable jump in drinking as well as urinating
  • A puppy who still cannot last an hour at four months old

The part most households get wrong

The interval is the easy half. The hard half is that in a house with three or four people, nobody is sure when the dog actually last went out. “She went out a couple of hours ago” frequently turns out to mean five. Common questions

How many times a day should a dog pee?

Three to five times a day is the normal range for a healthy adult dog. Puppies and seniors go more often. Consistently fewer than three, or more than six or seven, is worth mentioning to your vet.

Is it cruel to leave a dog 8 hours without a toilet break?

Overnight, no — a healthy adult dog sleeping through 8 hours is normal. During the day, 8 hours is too long: the RSPCA advises not leaving a dog alone for more than four hours at a stretch, and an awake, drinking, moving dog produces urine much faster than a sleeping one.

Does this work for cats?

No. Cats use a litter tray on their own schedule, and changes in a cat's urination — particularly straining in male cats — are an emergency. This calculator is for dogs only.
