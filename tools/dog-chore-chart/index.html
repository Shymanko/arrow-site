The short answer

Pick the jobs, type in who lives in your house, and this builds a one-page week chart with a name and a tick box in every slot. It rotates duties so the same person isn't on the 7am walk seven days running. Print it, stick it on the fridge. Nothing is uploaded anywhere — it all stays in your browser.

Rotating shifts each job by one person each day, so nobody gets stuck with the 7am walk every single morning.

Why a chart works when nagging doesn’t

The arguments in a dog-owning household are almost never about whether the dog should be walked. They are about whose turn it was, and whether it already happened. Those are information problems, and a chart on the fridge solves a surprising amount of both: it makes the work visible, and it removes the need for anyone to be the person who keeps track.

Two design choices matter more than the rest:

  • Put a name in every box, not just a tick box. An unassigned chore is everybody's and therefore nobody's. Assignment is the whole point.
  • Rotate. Fixed assignments quietly calcify into one person doing all the unpleasant slots. Rotating by day spreads the early mornings and the rainy evenings.

Giving jobs to children without setting them up to fail

Children can take on real responsibility for a dog, but the job has to match the age, and an adult remains accountable for the animal's welfare either way. A rough guide:

Age guidance adapted from Canine Journal and Nylabone; supervise until you have seen the child do it safely several times.
AgeCan genuinely do Needs an adult
2–4Put toys away, help refill the water bowl, learn gentle stroking Everything — they are learning by watching, not performing a chore
5–8Measure and pour food, brush, tidy supplies, hold the lead beside an adultFeeding (dogs can get snappy around food), all walks
9–12Feed independently with a reminder, help with baths, walk in a safe familiar area, spot signs something is offWalks near traffic, anything involving medication
13+Full responsibility for feeding and walking on known routes, yard clean-up, keeping the water topped upVet decisions, dosing, new or reactive dogs

One rule worth holding to regardless of age: medication never goes on a child's row. A missed or doubled dose is a different category of mistake from a missed brush.

Where paper runs out

A fridge chart is honest about who was supposed to do something. It is much worse at telling you what actually happened, because someone has to be standing in the kitchen to read it. The familiar failure: you are at work, you text “did anyone walk her?”, and you get three replies, two of which are questions.

That is the gap Arrow fills — the same rota, but it lives on everyone's phone, the reminder goes only to whoever is on duty, and when they mark it done it disappears for everybody at once.

Common questions

Is this chore chart really free?

Yes, and there is no sign-up. It runs entirely in your browser; the only thing stored is a copy of your own settings in your browser's local storage so the form remembers your household next time. Nothing is sent to us.

Can I print it?

Yes — the Print button strips the site navigation and prints the chart alone, which also works as “Save as PDF” in the print dialog. There is also a “Copy as text” button for pasting the week into a group chat.

What jobs should be on a dog chore chart?

At minimum: morning feed, evening feed, fresh water, morning walk, evening walk, and the last trip out before bed. Add medication if your dog takes any, plus whichever of yard clean-up, brushing, training and bowl-washing apply to your household. More than about ten rows and people stop reading it.

How do you split dog chores fairly between adults?

Split by slot rather than by task, and rotate the unpopular slots. One person always doing the early walk while another always does the easy evening one reads as unfair even when the minute counts match. Our guide on splitting dog care with a partner goes into the conversation itself.
