How much should I feed my dog?

Updated 2026-10-26 · Takes 20 seconds · Sourced & fact-checked · AI-assisted draft

The short answer

Feeding charts on the side of a bag are generic. The method vets actually use is an equation: RER = 70 × (weight in kg)0.75, multiplied by a life-stage factor to get daily calories. This calculator runs that equation, then converts the result into grams of your food using the calorie density printed on your bag — and reserves 10% for treats, because treats are part of the total, not extra.

These are the life-stage multipliers published in the Merck Veterinary Manual. Neutering genuinely lowers the requirement — it is not a myth.

Why the number on the bag is usually too much

Manufacturer feeding charts are built around a broad average, and they tend to be generous. An individual dog's real requirement can be as much as 50% above or below the typical figure for its weight, driven by metabolism, activity, neuter status and breed. A formula gets you closer than a chart, and your dog's own body gets you closer still.

Worked example: a 25 kg neutered Labrador. 250.75 = 11.18. × 70 = 783 kcal resting. × 1.6 = 1,253 kcal a day. Of that, up to 125 kcal can come from treats, leaving about 1,128 kcal of complete food — roughly 560 kcal per meal twice a day.

Calories are not grams

This is the step most online calculators skip, and it is the one that matters at the bowl. Dry dog foods range from roughly 300 to 500 kcal per 100 g. Feeding “300 g” of a dense food and 300 g of a light one differ by about a third of a day's calories. Find kcal/kg on your bag — by law it is there somewhere — and let the calculator do the conversion.

Weigh, don't scoop. The same “cup” filled by three different people in the same household varies by 20–30%. In a home where more than one person feeds the dog, a £10 kitchen scale removes more error than any choice of food will.

Then adjust to the dog, not the spreadsheet

Treat the calculator's answer as week one. Re-weigh every two to four weeks and run a hands-on body condition check. Ribs becoming hard to feel? Take about 10% off. Waist getting sharp and ribs too obvious? Add about 10%. Two to three weeks between changes — dogs do not respond to a new portion overnight, and chasing the scale weekly leads to overcorrection.

What this calculator will not do

It will not design a weight-loss plan. Restricting an overweight dog is calculated from a target weight and supervised by a vet, because taking weight off too fast is its own risk, and because obesity frequently has a treatable medical cause underneath it — hypothyroidism, for example. It also won't cover pregnant or nursing dogs, dogs recovering from illness, or anything on a prescription diet. Those all need a real consult.

One bowl, four people, four portions

Getting the number right is half the job. In a shared household the other half is making sure the dog gets that number once — not twice because two people both did the morning feed, and not zero because each assumed the other had.

Common questions

How much should I feed my dog by weight?

As a starting point, roughly 25–30 kcal per pound (55–65 kcal per kg) of body weight per day for an average adult dog. The RER/MER equations above are more accurate because they account for the fact that energy needs do not scale in a straight line with size — a 40 kg dog does not need twice what a 20 kg dog needs.

Should I feed my dog once or twice a day?

Twice a day suits most adult dogs. Puppies need more: about four meals a day from 6 to 12 weeks, three from 3 to 6 months, and two from 6 months onward. Deep-chested breeds in particular should not be fed one large daily meal.

Do treats count toward the daily total?

Yes. Treats, chews, training rewards and table scraps should together stay under 10% of daily calories, and that 10% comes out of the total, not on top of it. If you treat heavily on a training day, trim the meals a little.

Why does neutering change the number?

Neutering lowers metabolic rate, which is why the standard adult factor drops from 1.8 to 1.6 — a reduction of roughly 11%. Feeding a neutered dog the same amount as before the operation is one of the most common routes to gradual weight gain.
